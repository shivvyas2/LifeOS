Chapter seven

7Health

One tab for everything the body reports: how it is doing, and what it did. Two halves behind an underline picker, one week strip that moves both to the same day, and a rule that runs through every card on the screen: a missing reading is a dash, never a zero.

7.1Intent

Revision D called this tab Body and split it four ways: Activity, Weight, Recovery, Wellness. The restructure of 25 August folded those four into two halves that answer two different questions. Health is how the body is doing: recovery, sleep, vitals against their own baselines, weight, and the journal. Fitness is what it did: calories burnt, steps, strain, the sessions themselves, and the training week. The tab bar has five slots and this one takes one of them, because Almanac is still not a fitness app; it is the place a life's signals meet, and the body is one of nine sectors (Chapter 13).

Working backwards

Open Health and the day is already chosen for you: today, with a week strip to step back. Four tracking tiles across the top show steps, sleep, weight and recovery for that day, each a door to its own history. Below them, Health or Fitness. Health opens with anything that is off, then recovery and calories, a sleep card with the night's stages, HRV and resting heart rate, blood oxygen and skin temperature read against your own fortnight, and every other reading Apple Health had for the day, grouped and listed only where a value exists. Fitness opens with the week's burn as bars, then today's movement, strain and heart rates, the day's workouts, and a card that says how the training week is adding up with a row into the workout library.

Why does the whole tab move to one selected day?

Because both halves are readings of the same day. If Health kept Tuesday and Fitness kept Thursday, a reader could compare Tuesday's recovery with Thursday's session and draw a conclusion from a comparison that was never real. RootView owns one date and fans it out to all four view models at once.

Both my iPhone and Watch count steps. Does that double?

No. Steps arrive through HKStatisticsQuery with a cumulative-sum option, which de-duplicates overlapping samples across sources. Summing raw samples client-side would roughly double every step count on a day you carried both devices.

What does "everything Apple Health holds" mean in practice?

Forty-seven metrics in a vocabulary enum, read every day the sync touches. Ten of them have named columns on the day row because scorers and charts read them by name; the other thirty-seven live in a JSON bag on the same row. The screen lists whichever groups actually have a value, so a person with a blood pressure cuff sees blood pressure and a person without never learns the row existed.

Where does weight come from?

The Renpho scale, via Apple Health. Renpho already syncs there, so Almanac never talks to Renpho directly: one less integration, one less credential to store.

What the tab deliberately does not do

  • It never diagnoses. An anomaly banner says "Resting HR is well above your 2-week baseline" and stops. "See a doctor" is a claim this data cannot support.
  • It never invents a verdict from missing data. No sleep all week means no sleep verdict, not "Low".
  • It never colours strain with the recovery bands. High recovery is good and green; high strain is hard work, and green would read as praise. Strain gets one plain accent.

Four view models, one screen

Activity, Body, Recovery and Wellness keep their own folders even though none of them owns a tab any more. A section is a view concern; what each one derives is not. Merging them into one view model would produce a class that loads a day, a week, a fortnight and sixty days for four unrelated readings, and would reload all of it whenever any part changed. Four small loaders means a date change costs exactly four fetches and nothing else.

7.3Model

Every snapshot is a value type with optional fields. That is the rule the whole app is built on, enforced by the types rather than by discipline at each call site. A day with the watch on the charger produces nils, and every nil renders as a dash. 

// LIfeOS/Features/Recovery/Model/RecoverySnapshot.swift
struct RecoverySnapshot: Equatable {
    // The day
    var recoveryPct: Double?
    var hrvMs: Double?
    var restingHR: Double?
    var dayStrain: Double?

    // Sleep quality
    var sleepMinutes: Int?
    var sleepPerformancePct: Double?
    var sleepEfficiencyPct: Double?
    var sleepConsistencyPct: Double?
    var sleepDebtMinutes: Int?

    // Vitals, each read against its own baseline
    var spo2Percentage: Double?
    var skinTempCelsius: Double?
    var respiratoryRate: Double?

    // The fortnight
    var recoveryTrend = TrendSeries(points: [])
    var hrvTrend = TrendSeries(points: [])
    var spo2Trend = TrendSeries(points: [])
    // ... five more series, the nights, naps, anomalies, weekRecovery

    var hasAnyReading: Bool {
        recoveryPct != nil || sleepMinutes != nil || dayStrain != nil || hrvMs != nil
    }
}

The fortnight rides inside the day's snapshot because half of these readings mean nothing alone. An SpO2 of 97.2 tells a reader nothing; a tenth above their own baseline does. So every vital carries a TrendSeries, and the card renders the delta from that series' average rather than the bare number.

hasAnyReading is how the screens tell "not connected" apart from "connected and quiet". Without it the Connect Whoop card would show to someone whose strap simply had a slow night.

TrendSeries keeps its gaps

// LifeOSKit/Sources/DesignSystem/TrendSeries.swift
public struct TrendPoint: Sendable, Equatable, Identifiable {
    public let date: Date
    public let value: Double?      // nil is a day with no reading
}

public struct TrendSeries: Sendable, Equatable {
    public let points: [TrendPoint]
    public var hasAnyReading: Bool { points.contains { $0.value != nil } }
    public var average: Double? {   // mean of the readings that exist
        guard !readings.isEmpty else { return nil }
        return readings.reduce(0, +) / Double(readings.count)
    }
    public var latest: Double? {    // most recent reading, not the last slot
        points.reversed().first { $0.value != nil }?.value ?? nil
    }
    public var deltaFromAverage: Double? {
        guard readings.count > 1, let latest, let average else { return nil }
        return latest - average
    }
}

Three decisions live here. The average ignores gaps rather than counting them as zero. The latest reading is the last non-nil point, because the window usually ends on a day Whoop has not scored yet. And a series with one reading has no delta: a lone value is its own average, and "0 from baseline" would imply a history that has not been collected.

The day row and its two bags

DailyMetrics keeps its ten named health columns from Revision D (steps, exercise minutes, sleep minutes, active energy, weight, water, resting HR, HRV, SpO2, respiratory rate) and the Whoop columns. What is new is a long tail of Apple Health metrics that have no column of their own, and a record of who wrote each value. 

// LifeOSKit/Sources/Persistence/DailyMetrics.swift
/// Everything Apple Health can supply that does not have a column of its
/// own, as a JSON dictionary keyed by `HealthMetric.rawValue`.
public var extrasData: Data?

/// Who wrote each value, as a JSON dictionary keyed by `HealthMetric.rawValue`.
public var sourcesData: Data?

public func extra(_ key: String) -> Double? { extras[key] }
public func setExtra(_ key: String, _ value: Double?) {
    var bag = extras
    if let value { bag[key] = value } else { bag.removeValue(forKey: key) }
    extras = bag
}

Why a bag and not thirty-seven more columns: most people have no data for most of the tail, and a column each would mean a schema change per metric for fields nothing queries. The bag keeps adding a metric to one enum case and one mapping. The one thing a bag must not lose is the distinction this model is built on: a missing key means no reading, and writing nil removes the key rather than storing a zero. Both bags are optional so a store written before they existed opens without a migration, which matters after the lesson in 10.8.

sourcesData stores raw strings rather than a typed source because Persistence sits below Integrations and must not import it. The vocabulary lives up there.

The vocabulary

HealthMetric is its own enum, deliberately not a set of HealthKit types, so the file builds and tests without the framework. It carries nine groups (movement, heart and fitness, body, sleep, vitals, intake, mind, walking, cycle), a title, a unit label, a decimal count, and three merge properties the arbiter reads: 

// LifeOSKit/Sources/Integrations/HealthMetric.swift
/// Measured by a strap worn on the body, so a wearable outranks the phone.
public var claimedByWearable: Bool {
    switch self {
    case .restingHR, .hrvMs, .spo2Percentage, .respiratoryRate,
         .sleepMinutes, .deepSleepMinutes, .remSleepMinutes,
         .coreSleepMinutes, .awakeMinutes, .timeInBedMinutes,
         .sleepEfficiencyPercentage, .readinessScore:
        true
    default:
        false
    }
}

/// A person can type this into the app, so a background sync must not
/// replace what they entered on purpose.
public var acceptsManualEntry: Bool {
    switch self {
    case .weightKg, .waterML: true
    default: false
    }
}

/// Health is the top authority among syncing sources: nothing else measures
/// this and nobody types it, so the latest reading is simply the truth.
public var healthIsAuthoritative: Bool {
    !claimedByWearable && !acceptsManualEntry
}

Cycle metrics (basal temperature, menstrual flow) are their own group and are gated by needsCycleConsent. The first authorisation request never asks for them. Not because they are more private than a heart rate, but because a permission sheet that asks every person for menstrual data is telling most of them the app has misread who they are.

Recovery: one fetch, many readings

// LIfeOS/Features/Recovery/ViewModel/RecoveryViewModel.swift
let end = selectedDate
let start = calendar.date(byAdding: .day, value: -(Self.windowDays - 1), to: end) ?? end

// One fetch for the whole window. The selected day is picked out of
// it rather than fetched again.
let rows = try store.metrics(from: start, to: end)
let day = rows.first { calendar.isDate($0.date, inSameDayAs: end) }

let restingTrend = trend(rows, from: start, to: end) { $0.restingHR }

// History excludes the selected day: a spike must not drag the
// baseline toward itself on the very day it is being judged.
func history(_ series: TrendSeries) -> [Double?] {
    series.points.dropLast().map(\.value)
}

let anomalies = [
    AnomalyEvaluation.evaluate(metric: "Resting HR", today: day?.restingHR,
        history: history(restingTrend), threshold: .relativeAbove(0.10)),
    AnomalyEvaluation.evaluate(metric: "Skin temp", today: day?.skinTempCelsius,
        history: history(skinTrend), threshold: .absoluteAbove(1.0)),
    AnomalyEvaluation.evaluate(metric: "Blood oxygen", today: day?.spo2Percentage,
        history: history(spo2TrendSeries), threshold: .absoluteBelow(3.0)),
    AnomalyEvaluation.evaluate(metric: "HRV", today: day?.hrvMs,
        history: history(hrvTrendSeries), threshold: .relativeBelow(0.30)),
    // ... respiratory rate at .relativeAbove(0.08)
].compactMap { $0 }

The window is fourteen days because the Whoop sync window is fourteen days, so a chart never has a gap the sync could have filled. The trend helper builds a point for every calendar day in the window, not only the days with rows, which is what keeps a day Whoop never reported as a visible gap. Thresholds are relative where the metric scales with the person (heart rate, HRV) and absolute where it has a physiological unit (a degree of skin temperature means the same at any baseline). AnomalyEvaluation refuses to judge with fewer than seven readings in the history, because a noisy baseline raises false alarms.

Body: sixty days to find one number

// LIfeOS/Features/Body/ViewModel/BodyViewModel.swift
let start = calendar.date(byAdding: .day, value: -60, to: selectedDate) ?? .distantPast
let rows = try store.metrics(from: start, to: selectedDate)   // most-recent-first

let weighed = rows.compactMap { row in row.weightKg.map { (date: row.date, kg: $0) } }
var delta: Double?
if let newest = weighed.first,
   let weekAgo = weighed.first(where: { $0.date <= newest.date.addingTimeInterval(-6 * 86_400) }) {
    delta = newest.kg - weekAgo.kg
}

The hero is the most recent row that actually has a weight, which is not necessarily the most recent row. The delta is that weight minus the most recent weigh-in at least six days older; if none exists the delta is suppressed rather than computed over a shorter window, because a "weekly change" measured over two days is a wrong number wearing a right label. Sixty days rather than fourteen because people skip weeks, and a fortnight would suppress the delta for anyone who steps on the scale fortnightly.

Wellness: the journal is a note now

Revision D stored a journal entry as a PlanEntry of kind .journal. It is now a note page, one per day, in the notes store (Chapter 12). Writing twice in one evening appends to the day's page rather than filing a second entry, because two rows dated the same day would break the "did I write today" question the streak is built on. 

// LIfeOS/Features/Body/ViewModel/WellnessViewModel.swift
func addJournal(_ text: String) {
    let store = NotesStore(context: context, calendar: calendar)
    let entry = try store.journalEntry()
    var blocks = entry.blocks.filter { !($0.kind == .paragraph && $0.isEmpty) }
    blocks.append(contentsOf: NoteBlockParser.blocks(fromMarkdown: trimmed))
    try store.update(entry, blocks: blocks)
    load()
}

The same view model builds the Apple Health groups for the selected day. The week of rows above cannot answer "what did Health have for this day", so it fetches the one row separately and hands it to HealthDaySummary.groups(for:includingCycle:). The cycle switch it passes is the same UserDefaults key the sync reads, so what is on screen and what is being read can never disagree.

The connection view model

HealthConnectionViewModel is deliberately thinner than its Whoop counterpart: Health has no OAuth, no tokens and no server. What it does have is a permission model that refuses to tell the app anything, which shapes everything in it. 

// LIfeOS/Features/Settings/ViewModel/HealthConnectionViewModel.swift
enum State: Equatable {
    case unavailable          // iPad without Health, or a device that has none
    case notAsked             // never asked; the row offers to connect
    case syncing
    case synced(days: Int)    // days touched, not days with data
    case noData               // asked, and nothing came back
    case failed(String)
}

.noData deliberately does not say "denied". HealthKit never reports whether read access was granted, because admitting a refusal would leak that the user has no data of that type. Claiming a denial the app cannot see would be a guess presented as a fact, so the copy says "No data yet. Check Almanac in Health > Sharing" and points at the one place that knows.

The Fitness half

Calories first, as seven RoundedBarChart bars for the week ending on the selected day with the total in the header. Then steps and active time; strain, average HR and max HR; the day's workouts as WorkoutRows (only when there are any, so most days render no empty frame); the training card; and the trends link. The training card carries the way into the workout library with today's plan already on it (Chapter 10), but only when both the library view model and the recorder were handed down, because the player the library pushes records the session.

WorkoutRow shows a "partial" label when Whoop captured less than 95 percent of a session. A workout recorded at 40 percent is not an easy workout, and letting its low strain speak for it would say exactly that. It also shows the split chip when the session followed a catalog video, so a pull day in the library reads as a pull day in the list.

The fortnight screen

WhoopDetailScreen opens with SleepCompositionChart, one column per night, because it is the only chart showing structure rather than quantity: a short night and a fragmented night look identical on the duration chart and obviously different here, which is the reason the stages are ingested at all. Below it, five TrendCharts. 

// LIfeOS/Features/Recovery/View/TrendChart.swift
/// A run of consecutive days that all have readings.
///
/// Swift Charts joins a line straight across a day it was given no mark
/// for, which would draw a reading that never happened. Splitting the
/// window into runs and giving each its own series keeps the gap a gap.
private var segments: [Segment] {
    var runs: [Segment] = []
    var current: [TrendPoint] = []
    for point in series.points {
        if point.value != nil {
            current.append(point)
        } else if !current.isEmpty {
            runs.append(Segment(id: runs.count, points: current))
            current = []
        }
    }
    if !current.isEmpty { runs.append(Segment(id: runs.count, points: current)) }
    return runs
}

The chart is a line, not bars, because the shape of a fortnight is a direction and a line draws a direction. The chrome is almost absent: no gridlines, no y axis, two date labels in the footer. The y domain is the data's own range with a quarter of headroom, never anchored at zero, because a resting heart rate plotted from zero is a flat line with no shape in it. The current figure lives in the header in text ink, because the warmer module hues fall under 3:1 contrast on this surface and a visible label is the relief.

7.6How it works

Step by step: a foreground sync

  1. RootView comes to the foreground and calls health.startWatching(). If permission has ever been asked, the view model opens reader.changes(), an AsyncStream fed by one HKObserverQuery per universal metric type.
  2. Health records a batch of steps. The observer yields once; the stream buffers at most one event, so thirty batches landing together are one reason to re-read, not thirty.
  3. The view model runs sync(). If a sync is already in flight it awaits that one rather than starting a second: a launch task and a foreground transition arriving together do one pass.
  4. HealthSyncWindow.days(since:) decides which days to re-read: always today; the day of the last sync as well (a night's sleep is recorded against the morning it ends, so a day already covered can gain a value); thirty days on a first sync or after a gap that long; only today when the stamp is in the future, because that is a clock change, not a missing sync.
  5. For each day, reader.day(_:includingCycle:) runs one query per metric. Counts use .cumulativeSum; measurements use .discreteAverage, because summing a heart rate would produce a number in the thousands. Sleep is a category, not a quantity, so one HKSampleQuery answers all six sleep metrics by hand. A metric that errors is dropped rather than failing the day: one unreadable type must not cost the other forty-six.
  6. A day Health knows nothing about is skipped rather than written. An upsert would create an empty row and put a blank day on the calendar that nothing ever fills.
  7. HealthApply.write upserts the row. For each incoming value it reads the held value and the held source, asks MetricArbiter.resolve, writes the winner into the named column or the bag, and records the winning source.
  8. MetricsStore saves. ModelContext.didSave fires. RootView.reloadAll() reloads every snapshot. The tiles repaint through the path they already used; nothing here knows about a screen.
  9. The watch sleeps fifteen seconds before listening again.

The arbiter

// LifeOSKit/Sources/Integrations/MetricArbiter.swift
public static func resolve(
    metric: HealthMetric,
    existing: Double?, existingSource: MetricSource?,
    incoming: Double?, incomingSource: MetricSource,
    ranking: SourceRanking
) -> (value: Double?, source: MetricSource?) {
    // A source having no reading is not evidence of zero.
    guard let incoming else { return (existing, existingSource) }
    guard existing != nil else { return (incoming, incomingSource) }

    let held = existingSource ?? MetricSource.legacy(for: metric)

    // Greater than or equal, not greater than. Equal rank means the same
    // source syncing again, which must be allowed to correct itself.
    guard ranking.rank(incomingSource, for: metric) >= ranking.rank(held, for: metric) else {
        return (existing, held)
    }
    return (incoming, incomingSource)
}

Revision D merged with a boolean: fill gaps only. That could not tell a Whoop value from a typed one, which is exactly the distinction a second wearable forces. SourceRanking turns a source into a rank per metric: 4 for a typed value, 3 for the authority (Apple Health for passively counted metrics, the primary strap for wearable-claimed ones), 2 for a secondary reading, 1 for a fallback, 0 for unknown. The primary wearable defaults to Whoop, so an unset preference reproduces the old behaviour exactly.

One legacy rule matters: a value written before provenance existed reads as rank 0 for almost everything, so the next sync re-attributes it. Weight and water are the exception. A figure the user typed before this shipped is unattributed too, and treating it as unknown would let the next Health sync silently replace it. That is the one path that destroys data a person entered by hand, so an unattributed value for a typed metric is read as typed.

7.7Performance and cost

  • One fetch per view model per date change. Recovery fetches fourteen rows once and picks the selected day out of them. Activity fetches seven. Nothing fetches the same window twice.
  • Observer, not timer. A step count changes dozens of times an hour and not at all overnight. A polling interval short enough to look live wastes queries; one cheap enough to run is not live. HKObserverQuery fires when Health already knows something arrived.
  • Buffer of one, sleep of fifteen. The stream uses .bufferingNewest(1) and the consumer sleeps fifteen seconds after each pass. A day's steps arrive in dozens of batches, and re-reading forty-seven metrics for each one would spend the afternoon in HealthKit to move a numeral.
  • Foreground only. enableBackgroundDelivery needs an entitlement and wakes the app to write rows nobody is looking at. The launch and foreground syncs already cover the time the app was away. stopWatching() runs on every scene deactivation so a phone in a pocket is not running ten queries a quarter minute.
  • Bounded backfill. Thirty days is the month the dot grid shows. Going further would spend forty-seven queries a day on history no screen displays, and Health keeps the data regardless.
  • Every observer completes. The handler calls completion() on the error path too. HealthKit retries a query whose handler never completed, and a silent leak of those is how an app ends up with a hundred live observers.
  • The actor. HealthKitReader is an actor because HKHealthStore is a shared resource and the sync can be entered from two places at once. The observer array is actor state rather than a local because HKObserverQuery is not Sendable.
  • Nothing leaves the device. Apple Health readings are written to the local store and never synced to Supabase. The only health figures that ever leave are the three a person chooses to share with friends (Chapter 16).
  • No chart draws from nothing. TrendChart returns no view at all when a series has no reading, so a fresh install renders no empty axes.
A unit bug worth remembering HKUnit.percent() yields 0.98 for a 98 percent blood oxygen reading. The app stores percentages as percentages everywhere else, Whoop included, so a Health-sourced SpO2 was landing two orders of magnitude below a Whoop-sourced one and rendering as "1.0". It went unnoticed because Whoop owned the field and Health only filled its gaps. HealthKitReader.scale(for:) now multiplies every percent-unit metric by 100 on the way in.

Tests

  • MetricArbiterTests (10 tests): a missing reading never clears a value; Health does not overwrite Whoop; Health fills a Whoop gap; Health overwrites its own counts; a hand-entered value survives; the chosen strap overwrites the other; the secondary strap still fills a gap; an unattributed typed value is treated as typed.
  • HealthApplyTests (9 tests) against a real in-memory store: every column fills; Whoop values survive a Health sync; a later sync corrects a step count; counts are stored as integers; a write records its provenance; the long tail is attributed too. These exist because the arbiter can be perfectly correct and still be wired into the wrong column.
  • WhoopArbitrationTests (5): Whoop owns what it measures, does not overwrite the phone's exercise minutes or a typed weight, yields to the chosen strap.
  • HealthSyncWindowTests (8): a first sync backfills a month; today is always included; a recent sync re-reads only today; a gap is backfilled including the day before it; a very old sync is capped; the window never reaches the future.
  • HealthMetricVocabularyTests and CycleConsentTests: every metric has a title and group; raw values are unique; the first request never asks for cycle data; universal and cycle partition every metric.
  • AnomalyEvaluationTests (10): the baseline needs seven readings; relative and absolute thresholds flag at the boundary and stay quiet below it.
  • TrendSeriesTests (13) and SleepCompositionTests (6): averages ignore gaps; a single reading has no delta; points keep their gaps; a night with no stages is not renderable; awake counts toward the bar but not toward sleep.
Still not covered The four feature view models have no tests, for the reason recorded in the decision log: they take a context through attach rather than a protocol through init. The delta suppression in BodyViewModel and the verdict thresholds in WellnessViewModel (420 sleep minutes reads "Optimal", 20 exercise minutes counts as a workout day, 4 workout days reads "Consistent") are the ones most worth writing first.

Check yourself

  • Why does the arbiter use greater-than-or-equal rather than greater-than? Equal rank means the same source syncing again, and a source must be allowed to correct itself, which is how an afternoon sync fixes the morning's step count.
  • Why is a Health-sourced SpO2 multiplied by 100? HKUnit.percent() returns a fraction; the app stores percentages as percentages, and the mismatch rendered "1.0" for a 98 percent reading.
  • What does the observer stream's buffer size of one buy? A batch of samples is one reason to re-read the day, not thirty, and a throttled consumer never returns to a queue of stale events.
  • Why does the recovery baseline exclude the selected day? A spike on the day being judged would drag its own baseline toward itself and hide itself.
  • Why is a legacy unattributed weight treated as typed? It is the one path where a sync could destroy a value a person entered by hand.
  • Why does the app never say Health access was denied? HealthKit deliberately hides the answer, so any claim of denial would be a guess presented as fact.
