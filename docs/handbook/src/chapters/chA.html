Appendix A

AProduct thesis and roadmap

Why the app exists, in the words that were used before any of it was built, and where it stands on 15 September 2026: what shipped in the three weeks since Revision D, and what is still open.

A.1The thesis

Every health app you already have is a silo. Whoop knows your recovery but not that you skipped breakfast. Apple Health knows your steps but not that you blew the grocery budget and slept badly because of it. Each one gives advice that is correct in isolation and useless in context.

Almanac is built on a single bet: the value is in the join, not the collection. A coach that can see you missed meditation, logged five hours of sleep, wrote "low focus" in a note, and went over budget can say something no single-purpose app can. That is the entire reason to build one app instead of using five.

Everything in the architecture follows from that bet. There is one table keyed by date that every source writes into and every reader reads from. The nine sectors of the Life board exist to give the join a shape a person can act on: not "here is a chart" but "here is the one lever that still moves this month." And LIFO, the coach, is handed the join already summed rather than being asked to reason over rows, which is what makes it affordable, private, and fast.

A.2Product PR FAQ

Working backwards

Almanac is a personal almanac of your own life. It pulls together everything your devices already know about you: weight from your scale, recovery from your strap, movement from your watch, spending from your bank, the day from your calendar, and what you wrote down. It answers one question every morning, am I on track, and one question every month, where did the month go. Open it and a grid of dots shows the month at a glance. Ask LIFO anything and it answers from your numbers, out loud if you like. Nine cards score the parts of your life that matter, and the ones no device can measure get asked, not guessed.

Who is this for?

It began as one person's app, sideloaded on the author's own devices, and that constraint shaped the architecture. It is now on TestFlight with accounts, friends and groups, and the load-bearing assumption has shifted from "one user" to "every user's data stays on their own phone." The server holds what has to be shared or what has to survive a reinstall, and nothing else.

Why not just use Apple Health?

Apple Health is an excellent store and a poor interpreter. It will show you a chart of anything and tell you the meaning of nothing. Almanac reads from it, then adds the layer Health deliberately does not: a definition of a good day, a streak that survives days you forgot to log, a monthly score with the evidence behind it, and a coach that reads across domains.

Does my data go to a server?

Your health rows, your transactions and your sector scores never do. Your notes, your profile, and the three figures you choose to share with friends do, under row-level security that names friendship in the policy. When LIFO answers from the cloud it is handed derived numbers, never a heart-rate series, and every answer carries a disclosure you can open that shows exactly what was sent. Chapter 1 draws the boundary; Chapter 14 enforces it.

What is explicitly not in this product?

Screen time analysis. It was requested and it cannot be built, because Apple's API forbids reading the values into the app; see Chapter 19. Food logging by photo, which Revision D promised, has not been built and is not scheduled. Guest mode was removed: a store belongs to an account.

What is the hardest part?

Not the integrations. It is friction: any app that requires daily manual logging dies in about eleven days. Every product decision here is subordinate to keeping the daily cost near zero, which is why a note is one line into an Inbox, a workout is one tap on a video, and the sectors that need a human answer ask two questions a month, not twenty.

What does it cost to run?

Under two dollars per user per month, by design and by a ledger in the database. Chapter 24 has the arithmetic and the two lines that are not yet metered.

Why the cheap modules were not an afterthought The Life board, the workout library and the nudges are each one or two SwiftData models, one hue, one feature folder and a handful of pure functions in the package, assembled from components that already existed. They were cheap because the foundations were built properly: the design system, the daily spine, the MVVM template, the preview harness. They also carry disproportionate value, because every domain added to the join makes the coach materially better at its job.

What is the one bet every architectural decision follows from?

The value is in the join, not the collection: one table keyed by day, every source writes in, every reader reads out.

Which Revision D promise was never built, and why does it not matter to the architecture?

The Cloud Run API backbone; Edge Functions carried every server job that arrived, including long Whoop syncs under a lease.

Which open item is a cost problem as much as a privacy one?

Moving ElevenLabs behind an Edge Function: the key in the bundle has no per-user cap.

What changed about the load-bearing assumption between Revision D and E?

From "one user, no accounts" to "many accounts, each person's data on their own phone"; the server holds only what must be shared or must survive a reinstall.
