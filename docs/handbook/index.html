Life OS Engineering Handbook

A personal iOS app that unifies every signal from the devices you already wear and carry — Apple Watch, iPhone, Whoop, Renpho — and reasons over all of it to say what's working and what isn't.

Rev A · 10 Aug 2026 iOS 26 · Swift 6 Status: V1 in build Audience: personal

This handbook is the single reference for how Life OS is built, why it is built that way, and what it does when things go wrong. It documents a system that is designed but not yet finished — V1 is in active implementation. Where the code and this document disagree, the code is right and this document is a bug.

Part I

Product

The thesis

Every health app you already have is a silo. Whoop knows your recovery but not that you skipped breakfast. Apple Health knows your steps but not that you blew the grocery budget and slept badly because of it. Each one gives advice that is correct in isolation and useless in context.

Life OS is built on a single bet: the value is in the join, not the collection. A coach that can see you missed meditation, logged 5 hours of sleep, journalled "low focus," and went 900 calories over can say something no single-purpose app can. That is the entire reason to build one app instead of using five.

Everything in the architecture follows from that bet. There is one table keyed by date that every source writes into and every reader reads from. Cheap manual modules ship early precisely because they make the coach smarter, not despite being simple.

Working backwards · Product

Life OS

Life OS is a personal dashboard that pulls together everything your devices already know about you — weight from your scale, recovery from your strap, movement from your watch, and what you ate from a photo — and answers one question every morning: am I on track? Open it and a grid of dots shows the month at a glance. Filled means a good day. The app knows what a good day means because you told it.

Who is this for?

One person. This is a personal app, sideloaded on the author's own devices. There are no accounts, no other users, and no App Store distribution. That constraint removes an enormous amount of architecture and is load-bearing throughout this document.

Why not just use Apple Health?

Apple Health is an excellent store and a poor interpreter. It will show you a chart of anything and tell you the meaning of nothing. Life OS reads from it, then adds the layer Health deliberately doesn't: a definition of a good day, a streak that survives days you forgot to log, and eventually a coach that reads across domains.

What is explicitly not in this product?

Screen time analysis. It was requested and it cannot be built — see Screen Time for the technical reason. Rather than ship a hollow version, it is cut.

What's the hardest part?

Not the integrations. It's friction: any app that requires daily manual logging dies in about eleven days. Every product decision here is subordinate to keeping the daily cost near zero — which is why food logging is a photo, not a form.

Working backwards · V1 feature

Today

Today is the hub. It opens with the date at the size of a poster, a strip of the current week, and a grid of dots — one per day this month. Filled dots are days you hit your targets, hollow dots are days you didn't, and today is orange. Underneath, four tiles: steps, sleep, weight, recovery. You can read the whole month in about a second without processing a single number.

What makes a dot fill?

Meeting 3 of 4 daily goals: steps ≥ 8,000, sleep ≥ 7h, exercise ≥ 30 min, water ≥ 2,500 ml. All five values — the four goals and the required count — are editable in Settings. The rule is deliberately explicit; a vague definition makes the entire grid meaningless.

What happens on a day with no data at all?

It renders as no-data, not as a miss, and it is excluded from streak arithmetic entirely. Leaving your watch on the charger is not a moral failure, and a system that punishes it teaches you to distrust the streak.

And a day with some data?

Judged on what it has. If steps are logged at 500 and nothing else was recorded, that's a miss. The alternative — treating partial days as blank — would let forgetting to log water silently launder a bad day into a neutral one.

If I change a goal, does history change?

Yes. Status is computed on read, never stored, so past dots re-evaluate against the new target immediately. This is intentional: the grid answers "how am I doing against my current standard," not "how did I do against a standard I've since abandoned."

Working backwards · V1 feature

Body

Body is a teal screen with your weight at 96 points and nothing competing with it. Below, the week's change with direction, and a fourteen-day bar chart where each bar is a weigh-in. Days you didn't step on the scale are visibly gaps, not interpolations.

Where does weight come from?

Your Renpho scale, via Apple Health. Renpho already syncs there, so Life OS never talks to Renpho directly — one less integration, one less thing to break, and no third-party credentials to store.

Why show a weekly delta instead of a trend line?

Daily bodyweight is mostly water and noise. A seven-day delta is the shortest window where the signal beats the noise, and it's the number that actually changes behaviour.

What if I weigh myself twice in one day?

The row for that day holds one weight; the most recent write wins. This is a deliberate simplification — for a daily-granularity app, the second weigh-in is the better one anyway.

Working backwards · V1 feature

Activity

Activity is amber, with today's step count as the hero and three tiles beneath it: active minutes, energy burned, and resting heart rate. It answers "did I move today" before you've finished unlocking the phone.

Both my iPhone and Watch count steps. Does that double?

No. Life OS uses HKStatisticsCollectionQuery, which de-duplicates overlapping samples across sources. Summing raw samples client-side — the obvious naive approach — would roughly double every step count on days you carried both devices.

Why is resting heart rate on the Activity screen and not Recovery?

Because it comes from HealthKit and is available in V1, while Recovery's other metrics depend on the Whoop integration landing. It moves to Recovery once that ships.

Working backwards · V1 feature

Recovery

Recovery is blue, and leads with Whoop's recovery percentage — the number you'd otherwise open the Whoop app to see — with HRV, day strain, and sleep performance beneath it.

Whoop syncs to Apple Health. Why build an OAuth integration at all?

Whoop writes sleep, workouts, and heart rate to Health, but not Recovery % or Day Strain — those are proprietary and exist only through Whoop's own API. They are also the two numbers most people wear the strap for. Hence the integration.

What does this screen show before Whoop is connected?

A designed empty state — an em dash and "Connect Whoop in Settings" — never 0%. A zero recovery score is a medically alarming number to display to someone who simply hasn't logged in yet.

What if I didn't wear the strap last night?

Whoop returns no recovery record for that cycle, the field stays nil, and the screen shows the same empty state. No interpolation, no carry-forward from yesterday.

Working backwards · V2 feature

The Coach

Every morning, one card: what you did well yesterday, what slipped, and one thing to fix today. Built from your actual weight, sleep, recovery, steps, calories, and water. Ask it anything — "why is my recovery tanking?" — and it queries your real records to answer rather than guessing.

Does my health data go to a server?

For the coach, no. It runs on Apple's on-device Foundation Models — free, private, offline, no API key. Only food photos leave the device, because the on-device model is text-only and cannot see an image.

How does it avoid making numbers up?

Tool calling. The model doesn't receive your data in a prompt; it calls Swift functions that query DailyMetrics and returns what they hand back. A number it had to look up is a number it cannot hallucinate — and the context stays small enough for a 3B on-device model to handle.

What if the on-device model isn't available?

SystemLanguageModel.availability is checked at runtime. On a device without Apple Intelligence, or while the model is still downloading, the coach degrades to unavailable rather than silently shipping health data to a cloud provider without asking.

Part II

Architecture

System context

Four devices produce data, two of them reach the app through Apple Health, one through a REST API, and one — your MacBook — cannot reach it at all. Everything converges on a single local store before anything renders.

Module map

LifeOSKit is a local Swift package providing compile-time boundaries. The app target contains only screens and wiring. The dependency rules are enforced by the compiler, not by convention.

RuleEnforced howWhy
Nothing outside HealthData imports HealthKitPackage dependency graphHealthKit types never leak into views; the module is swappable and testable
Nothing outside WhoopKit knows Whoop existsPackage dependency graphA vendor change is contained to one target
Nothing outside SyncKit imports the Supabase SDKPackage dependency graphThe app works identically with sync disabled
Insights reads Persistence onlyPackage dependency graphThe coach cannot make network calls mid-conversation
DesignSystem depends on nothingPackage dependency graphComponents stay previewable and reusable across every module

The DailyMetrics spine

This is the single most consequential decision in the system. One row per calendar day. Every source writes into it; every reader reads from it.

What the single-table choice buys

  • Trends become a sort, not a multi-source asynchronous join.
  • The coach queries one table instead of orchestrating two network APIs mid-conversation — which is what makes a 3B on-device model viable.
  • Offline works by default, because nothing renders from a live network call.
  • Adding a source later means writing into the same row, not building a new pipeline.

Local-first topology

SwiftData on-device is the source of truth for the UI. Supabase is backup, sync, and a place to run jobs that a sleeping phone cannot.

Conflict resolution

Field-level last-write-wins, using updatedAt. Not row-level.

Row-level LWW would be a bug here. The nightly Whoop job writes recovery fields at 04:00; if you logged water at 23:00 the night before, a row-level overwrite silently discards it. Field-level merge means each writer touches only its own columns.

This is defensible only because there is exactly one human user and the writers rarely contest the same field: HealthKit owns the health columns, the Whoop job owns the Whoop columns, and you own the manual ones. If this app ever became multi-user, this strategy would need replacing outright.

Known limitation LWW depends on clock agreement between the device and the Edge Function. Device clock skew of more than a few minutes can cause a stale write to win. Accepted for a single-user personal app; documented so it isn't rediscovered as a mystery bug.

Integrations

The primary source. Read-only in V1 — the app requests no write permissions, which keeps the authorization surface minimal and means Life OS can never corrupt your Health data.

Freshness

  • HKAnchoredObjectQuery with a persisted anchor per type, so each sync fetches only what changed since last time.
  • enableBackgroundDelivery so iOS wakes the app when new samples land.
  • HKStatisticsCollectionQuery for daily aggregates — this de-duplicates across sources. Summing raw samples yourself double-counts steps on any day you carried both an iPhone and a Watch.
The authorization trap HealthKit read authorization is deliberately opaque. The API will not tell you whether the user denied a type — only whether you have asked. Branching on read authorization status is therefore always a bug. Query anyway, and treat an empty result as "no data," rendering an explicit empty state. Apple designed it this way so apps cannot detect that you are hiding a condition from them.

Whoop

Base URL https://api.prod.whoop.com, API v2. v1 is deprecated. Note that v2 identifies sleep by UUID rather than integer ID, and recovery webhooks reference the associated sleep UUID, not the cycle ID — a migration footgun worth knowing before you read the payloads.

Scopes

offline
read:cycles
read:sleep
read:recovery
read:workout
read:body_measurement
read:profile

offline is required to receive a refresh token. Without it, the integration breaks the first time the access token expires.

OAuth — and why the secret never ships

Whoop's token exchange requires a client secret. Embedding it in the app binary would make it extractable with strings. Supabase Edge Functions solve this cleanly: the app does PKCE and obtains a code; the function exchanges it.

Nightly job and webhooks

  • A scheduled Edge Function (pg_cron) pulls the previous day's cycle, recovery, and sleep overnight, so morning data exists before you open the app.
  • A whoop-webhook function receives push updates. Signature verified against WHOOP_WEBHOOK_SECRET; unverified requests are rejected outright.
  • Webhooks are only possible because a public HTTPS endpoint now exists. A phone alone cannot receive them.

Supabase

Schema

Mirrors the local SwiftData models: daily_metrics, workout_records, sleep_records, whoop_raw, and sync_state.

whoop_raw stores unprocessed API payloads. This is deliberate: when Whoop's response shape differs from the documentation — and it will — you can re-derive every downstream value without re-fetching a month of history against a rate limit.

Row Level Security

RLS is enabled on every table, without exception Policies scope all access to auth.uid(). This is precisely what makes shipping the anon key inside the app safe. An RLS misconfiguration does not throw — it returns an empty result set, which looks exactly like "no data yet." Verify policies with a real query before trusting an empty screen.

Foundation Models

The coach runs on Apple's on-device model by default. All three tiers sit behind the same LanguageModelSession API, so routing is configuration rather than a rewrite.

TierUsed forContextCostAvailable
SystemLanguageModelDaily brief, chat, trend commentarySmallFree, private, offlineiOS 26
PrivateCloudComputeLanguageModelMonthly / quarterly deep analysis32KFree, privateiOS 27

iOS 27 additionally introduces public LanguageModel and LanguageModelExecutor protocols, letting third-party providers implement the same interface. Do not hand-roll a provider abstraction — Apple ships one, and a bespoke layer would have to be torn out.

Tool calling is the anti-hallucination mechanism The model is not handed your data in a prompt. It calls Swift functions that query DailyMetrics. Two consequences: a number it had to look up is a number it cannot invent, and the context stays small enough for an on-device model to handle a month of history.

Screen Time — why it is cut

Screen time was an explicit product requirement. It cannot be built as specified, and the reason is worth documenting precisely so it is not re-attempted.

  • iPhone / iPad: a screen-time view is possible; screen-time analysis alongside health data is not. It would also require Apple to grant the Family Controls distribution entitlement.
  • MacBook: no API exists at any privilege level. Manual entry only, if ever.

Since the entire product thesis is the join across domains, a signal that provably cannot enter the join has no home here. Cut rather than half-built.

Every metric is optional, and that is not negotiable A missing value and a zero must never be representable as the same thing. Zero steps means you didn't move; nil steps means the watch was on the charger. In a health app a false zero is worse than a blank — it is a wrong number presented with total confidence.

Source records

WorkoutRecord and SleepRecord hold data that is genuinely not daily. They roll up into DailyMetrics, which means the daily row is always safe to recompute and never the only copy of anything.

Goal evaluation

public func evaluate(_ reading: DayReading,
                     against targets: GoalTargets) -> DayStatus

Returns .onTarget, .missed, or .noData.

InputResultRationale

Expects most-recent-first ordering. .noData days are transparent — they neither extend nor break a streak. .missed terminates immediately.

MetricsStore

The only path by which anything writes to DailyMetrics. It enforces the one-row-per-day invariant so callers cannot get it wrong.

@discardableResult
func upsert(date: Date, apply: (DailyMetrics) -> Void) throws -> DailyMetrics
func metrics(from: Date, to: Date) throws -> [DailyMetrics]   // most-recent-first
func goals() throws -> UserGoals                              // creates defaults on first access

upsert normalises any timestamp to startOfDay and merges — fields the closure doesn't touch are preserved. That is what lets HealthKit and Whoop write the same row safely.

Operations

Setup runbook

git clone https://github.com/shivvyas2/LifeOS.git

In Xcode: Swift Language Version → 6, Minimum Deployments → iOS 26.0, and add LifeOSKit via Add Package Dependencies → Add Local.

supabase init

                     WHOOP_WEBHOOK_SECRET=...
supabase functions deploy whoop-token-exchange
supabase functions deploy whoop-sync
supabase functions deploy whoop-webhook

Then put the project URL and anon key into Config/Secrets.xcconfig.

3 · Whoop developer app

  1. Register at developer.whoop.com (requires an active Whoop membership).
  2. Redirect URI: lifeos://whoop/callback
  3. Request all seven scopes listed in Whoop, including offline.
  4. Client ID → Config/Secrets.xcconfig. Client secret → supabase secrets set, never the xcconfig.
  5. Webhook URL → the deployed whoop-webhook function URL.

4 · Device

HealthKit background delivery and the Whoop OAuth round trip cannot be verified in the simulator. Both require a physical device with a signing team configured.

Secrets

There is no runtime .env on iOS. The app is a signed bundle; anything shipped inside it is extractable. The governing rule is therefore: what must never leak, must live server-side.

TierLocationContentsIn git?
1 · Build config Config/Secrets.xcconfig Supabase URL, anon key, Whoop client ID Gitignored. Template committed
2 · Runtime Keychain Whoop access + refresh tokens, Supabase session Never
3 · Server Edge Function env WHOOP_CLIENT_SECRET, WHOOP_WEBHOOK_SECRET, ANTHROPIC_API_KEY, SERVICE_ROLE_KEY Never. Never on device either

Nothing in Tier 1 is truly secret. The Supabase anon key is designed to ship in clients — it is safe precisely because RLS gates every row — and the Whoop client ID is public by OAuth design. Tier 1 is gitignored for hygiene, not because a leak would be catastrophic.

service_role is the one that matters It bypasses RLS entirely. It belongs only in an Edge Function environment — never in an xcconfig, never in the app target, never in git. Every other secret in this system is recoverable by rotation; this one grants total read and write access to everything.

Testing strategy

Logic that can be tested without a simulator lives in the package and is developed test-first. SwiftUI view bodies are verified by previews and a build — asserting on view structure is ceremony that breaks on every layout change without catching real defects.
